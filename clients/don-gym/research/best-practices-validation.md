## Claim 1: Lead Speed-to-Contact

**Deck claim:** "Leads convert ~4x more often when followed up within 10 minutes" and "most boutique-gym leads go cold within ~72 hours."

### Verdict: DIRECTIONALLY TRUE BUT NUANCE

**The real numbers:**

The "4x within 10 minutes" framing does not appear in the primary studies. The actual research findings are:

- **21x higher qualification rate** when contacted within 5 minutes vs. 30 minutes (Harvard Business Review / MIT study, 2011, James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, David Elkington — analyzed 2,241 US companies and 100,000+ web-generated leads)
- **100x higher contact rate** within 5 minutes vs. 30 minutes (same study)
- **9x higher conversion** within 5 minutes (MIT companion research)
- **8x greater conversion** in the first 5 minutes (InsideSales 2021 Lead Response Research, 55 million sales activities, 5.7 million inbound leads at 400+ companies)
- **7x more likely to qualify** if contacted within 1 hour vs. waiting longer (same HBR study)

**The "4x / 10-minute" framing is a simplified derivative** — likely drawn from "400% decrease in odds going from 5 to 10 minutes," which gets repackaged as "4x." That phrasing is secondary interpretation, not a primary study finding. The primary studies use 5-minute vs. 30-minute windows, not 10-minute windows.

**The 72-hour cold-lead claim:** Supported directionally. IHRSA data shows boutique studios close 28–35% of qualified leads with same-day follow-up vs. under 12% for leads waiting 48+ hours. The average fitness studio response time is 47 hours (Mindbody Business 2025 benchmark), meaning most studios are already near or past the danger window. "72 hours" is conservative — leads are substantially degraded by 24–48 hours, not just 72.

**Recommended deck language:**

> "Research from Harvard Business Review and InsideSales shows contacting a lead within 5 minutes produces 8–21x better conversion than waiting 30 minutes. For fitness studios, leads followed up same-day close at nearly 3x the rate of leads waiting 48+ hours."

**Sources:**

- Harvard Business Review / MIT 2011 study: [ainora.lt compilation](https://ainora.lt/blog/lead-response-time-statistics-every-study-2026)
- InsideSales 2021: [insidesales.com/response-time-matters](https://www.insidesales.com/response-time-matters/)
- Fitness-specific: [nutripy.io gym lead follow-up](https://nutripy.io/blog/gym-lead-follow-up-automation)

## Claim 2: Retention vs. Acquisition Cost (5–7x)

**Deck claim:** "It costs 5–7x more to acquire a new member than to retain one."

### Verdict: DIRECTIONALLY TRUE BUT NUANCE (partially a marketing myth)

**The real story:**

The "5x" figure traces back to a **1990 Harvard Business Review article by Frederick Reichheld** ("Zero Defections: Quality Comes to Services") based on credit card and insurance industry data — not fitness, and not modern digital businesses. The statistic has been widely repeated for 35 years with minimal scrutiny.

**Investigative finding:** A 2023 analysis by Hashtag Paid found the claim "is more myth than confirmed fact" — describing the citation chain as a "game of telephone" where sources cite sources with no primary data. The original Bain Capital research from 2003 cited a ~4x ratio for banking.

**What's defensible today:**

- The realistic range, per modern analysis, is **3x to 25x** depending on industry, business model, and customer segment
- For boutique fitness specifically, the ratio is supported qualitatively — studios with paid lead gen spend $40–150+ per lead acquisition; retention-focused spending (loyalty programs, onboarding, check-ins) is materially cheaper per retained member
- The **7x figure** in various citations refers not to acquisition cost but to **customer behavior** (loyal customers are 7x more likely to try a new product offering) — a different claim

**The "5–7x" range remains usable in the deck as an order-of-magnitude directional claim, not a precision statistic.** Flag it as industry rule-of-thumb, not a citable academic finding.

**Recommended deck language:**

> "Industry rule of thumb: retaining a member costs 3–5x less than acquiring a new one. For ARCH, this means every member who stays another 6 months is worth more than a fresh ad campaign."

**Sources:**

- Myth analysis: [hashtagpaid.com deep-dive](https://hashtagpaid.com/banknotes/does-it-still-cost-5x-more-to-acquire-customers-than-to-retain-them-in-2023)
- Invesp data: [invespcro.com acquisition vs retention](https://www.invespcro.com/blog/customer-acquisition-retention/)
- Range context: [churnkey.co acquisition vs retention guide](https://churnkey.co/blog/customer-acquisition-vs-retention-cost-comparison-guide/)

## Claim 3: Meta Ad Quality Ranking (30–40% CPM Reduction)

**Deck claim:** "Above-average ad quality ranking pays 30–40% less per impression than below-average; quality ranking is driven by engagement signals."

### Verdict: DIRECTIONALLY TRUE BUT NUANCE

**What Meta officially says:**
Meta's Help Center confirms that Quality Ranking measures perceived ad quality vs. ads competing for the same audience, driven by engagement signals: people hiding the ad, negative feedback, sensationalized language, and engagement bait. In 2019, Meta retired the single Relevance Score and replaced it with three separate rankings: Quality Ranking, Engagement Rate Ranking, and Conversion Rate Ranking.

**Meta does not publish an official CPM-reduction percentage** in its Help Center documentation. The "30–40% less per impression" figure appears in practitioner analysis and AI-marketing tool marketing material (e.g., get-ryze.ai), not in a Meta-authored study.

**What is supportable:**

- Multiple ad practitioners report CPM reductions of 30–50% when improving from Below Average to Above Average across all three rankings simultaneously
- Meta's auction mechanics confirm that relevance signals directly affect winning bids — higher quality = lower cost to win the same impression
- The directional logic is correct and well-established: quality matters in Meta's auction

**The 30–40% range is a practitioner estimate, not an official Meta figure.** The actual range likely varies significantly by industry, audience, and competition level.

**Recommended deck language:**

> "Meta's ad auction rewards high-quality, engaging ads — advertisers with above-average quality rankings consistently pay materially less per impression (industry practitioners report 30–50% CPM reductions) than those with below-average rankings."

**Sources:**

- Meta official: [Meta Business Help Center — Quality Ranking](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/303639570334185)
- Practitioner analysis: [lineardesign.com relevance score guide](https://lineardesign.com/blog/facebook-relevance-score/)
- CPM context: [benly.ai CPM reduction guide](https://benly.ai/learn/meta-ads/meta-ads-cpm-too-high-fix)

## Claim 4: Boutique Gym Retention / Churn Benchmarks

**Deck claim:** (Implicit) Industry benchmarks for boutique/HYROX/strength gym retention, LTV, and monthly churn.

### Verdict: CONFIRMED (with source precision)

**Current benchmarks (use these — not older IHRSA figures):**

**Annual retention:**

- Industry average (all gyms): **66.4% annual retention** — HFA (formerly IHRSA) 2025 Fitness Industry Benchmarking Report (175 companies, 17,000+ facilities, 2024 data). The older 71.4% figure widely cited is from IHRSA's 2016 report using 2015 data — do not use.
- Boutique fitness studios: **~70% annual retention**, outperforming traditional big-box gyms

**Monthly churn:**

- Boutique studios: **3.3–4.5% monthly attrition**
- Traditional gyms: **4–6% monthly attrition**
- Industry overall: ~30–40% annual churn (inverse of retention)

**Member Lifetime Value (LTV):**

- Boutique fitness studios: **$1,800–$3,200 average LTV** (IHRSA 2023)
- Big-box gyms: $400–$900 average LTV
- No HYROX-specific benchmark found — use boutique range as proxy

**LTV calculation for ARCH:** At $150–200/month membership × 12–18 months average tenure = $1,800–$3,600 LTV range, consistent with boutique benchmarks

**Sources:**

- HFA 2025 Benchmarking Report via: [smarthealthclubs.com 100 stats](https://smarthealthclubs.com/blog/100-gym-membership-retention-statistics/)

## Claim 5: Organic-First Lowers Paid Acquisition Costs (Flywheel)

**Deck claim:** Strong organic engagement lowers paid acquisition costs for local businesses.

### Verdict: CONFIRMED (established practice, no single definitive study)

**What's supported:**

- High-organic-engagement content repurposed as paid ads consistently delivers **lower CPMs and CPAs** — "some clients report up to 40% lower CPMs using organic winners as ad creative" (Hootsuite, Sprout Social practitioner research)
- Meta's auction system directly rewards content that earns high engagement — organic posts that perform well carry those engagement signals into paid amplification
- The flywheel logic is structurally sound: organic builds audience → audience engagement improves ad relevance scores → improved relevance scores lower CPMs → lower CPMs enable more reach per dollar spent
- Sprout Social and Hootsuite both document the "organic + paid hybrid" as a best practice for maximizing ROI

**Nuance:** There is no single peer-reviewed study quantifying the exact multiplier for local fitness businesses specifically. The mechanism is real; the exact percentage depends on baseline content quality and audience size.

**Recommended deck language:**

> "Organic content that earns genuine engagement trains Meta's algorithm on what your audience responds to — that signal carries into paid campaigns, lowering your cost per lead over time."

**Sources:**

- [Hootsuite organic vs paid social](https://blog.hootsuite.com/organic-vs-paid-social-media/)
- [Sprout Social hybrid strategy](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/organic-vs-paid-social-media/)
- [Mailchimp organic vs paid overview](https://mailchimp.com/resources/organic-vs-paid-social-media/)

## Claim 6: Content Pillar / "1 Long-Form → 15+ Pieces" Repurposing Model

**Deck claim:** "1 long-form pillar → 15+ derivative pieces across channels"

### Verdict: CONFIRMED (well-established, Gary Vee's model says 30+)

**What the research shows:**

- Gary Vaynerchuk's Content Model (published 2018, widely adopted since) uses **1 pillar piece → 30+ derivative pieces** across 20+ platforms. The core mechanic is documented in his original PDF and on GaryVaynerchuk.com. The "15+" claim in the deck actually understates his published model.
- The pillar/cluster content strategy (separately from Gary Vee) is canonically documented by HubSpot, Brafton, SEMrush, and others — standard practice is **10–15 cluster pieces per pillar page**
- These are two overlapping but distinct frameworks: Gary Vee's is social-repurposing focused; HubSpot's pillar-cluster is SEO-architecture focused. Both are established best practices.

**The "15+" number is conservative and defensible.** You could accurately say "30+ pieces" citing Gary Vee directly.

**Recommended deck language:**

> "Gary Vaynerchuk's documented Content Model turns 1 pillar piece (long-form video, keynote, deep-dive post) into 30+ derivative pieces across platforms — short clips, quote cards, stories, articles, and ads."

**Sources:**

## Claim 7: AI-Search Readiness for Local Business (Three Sub-Claims)

### 7a: Blocking AI crawlers in robots.txt makes a business invisible to AI search

**Verdict: CONFIRMED WITH CRITICAL NUANCE**

The claim requires distinguishing between two entirely different bot categories:

| Bot Type                  | Examples                                     | What Blocking Does                                                    |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Training crawlers         | GPTBot, ClaudeBot, CCBot                     | Stops training data collection; **does NOT affect AI search answers** |
| Search/retrieval crawlers | OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User | Blocking these **removes you from AI search answers**                 |

**The nuance matters:**

- Blocking GPTBot prevents OpenAI from using your content in model training — but **OAI-SearchBot** is what powers ChatGPT search answers. Blocking OAI-SearchBot removes your site from ChatGPT search results entirely.
- Anthropic has three separate crawlers: ClaudeBot (training), Claude-User (user queries), and Claude-SearchBot (search indexing). Blocking ClaudeBot has no effect on search visibility.
- 62–69% of sites currently block training bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot); 40–49% are also inadvertently blocking search bots

**For a local gym wanting discovery:** blocking training crawlers is a legitimate choice; blocking search crawlers is counterproductive. The robots.txt strategy matters and requires care.

### 7c: llms.txt as an emerging standard

**Verdict: SPECULATIVE — be honest about this in the deck**

**What's actually true:**

- llms.txt is a **community convention, not a formal standard** — no IETF RFC, no ISO standard
- **Anthropic and Perplexity** have publicly confirmed they support/read llms.txt; **OpenAI** has not confirmed; **Google has explicitly stated it does not support llms.txt** and is not planning to (Gary Illyes, July 2025); John Mueller compared it to the discredited keywords meta tag
- Overall web adoption: ~10% of domains (SE Ranking study of 300,000 domains)
- Early adopters are tech/developer-facing SaaS — not local service businesses
- For a local gym, llms.txt is a "takes 10 minutes, future insurance" move, not a proven conversion lever

**Recommended deck language:**

> "llms.txt is an emerging convention (not yet an industry standard) that helps AI assistants understand your site's content. It's supported by some AI platforms and takes 15 minutes to implement — low-effort, low-risk, and positions ARCH as an AI-ready brand."

**Do not present it as a confirmed standard or a significant traffic driver. Frame it as a forward-looking hygiene step.**

**Sources:**

- [linkbuildinghq.com should you implement llms.txt 2026](https://www.linkbuildinghq.com/blog/should-websites-implement-llms-txt-in-2026/)

## Claim 8: Local Multi-Location SEO (Own Page + Own GBP per Location)

**Deck claim:** Each physical location needs its own indexed page and its own verified Google Business Profile.

### Verdict: CONFIRMED (Google official policy)

**What Google says directly:**

- Google's own guidelines state: "If you have different locations for your service business, with separate service areas and separate staff at each location, you're allowed one profile for each location"
- Multi-location SEO best practice (per Google Search Central, Moz, Ahrefs, and Semrush): each location requires a unique URL, unique NAP (Name/Address/Phone) matching the GBP, and 60–70% unique content per page

**For ARCH (currently single location but potentially expanding):** even for a single location, a dedicated location page with embedded map, local testimonials, and schema markup is the correct foundation.

**Recommended deck language:** The current claim is accurate as stated. No revision needed.

## Claim 9: SMS Follow-Up for Fitness + 10DLC Registration

### 9a: SMS materially higher open/response rate than email

**Verdict: CONFIRMED**

The SMS vs. email open rate gap is one of the most consistently cited statistics in marketing and is supported across multiple independent sources:

- **SMS open rate: ~98%** (Optimonk April 2026, Sender March 2026, Omnisend 2026)
- **Email open rate: ~20%** (Campaign Monitor industry average; fitness/health sector slightly higher at 21–24%)
- **SMS response rate: 45%** vs. **email response rate: 10%**
- **90% of SMS read within 3 minutes**
- Fitness/wellness sector specific: 25–35% SMS response rate (TextUs SMS Benchmarks for Fitness and Wellness, December 2025)
- Studios with automated SMS follow-up under 5 minutes close **3x more trials** than those relying on email

**Note:** The 98% open rate figure is an industry-wide average; some researchers note it includes transactional texts (delivery confirmations, etc.) which inflate the number. For marketing SMS specifically, open rates are still far higher than email but may be closer to 85–90% in some studies. The directional claim holds strongly.

### 9b: 10DLC registration is required for US business A2P SMS

**Verdict: CONFIRMED — and now mandatory, not just recommended**

**Current status (February 2025 enforcement):**

- **Unregistered 10DLC numbers are now blocked by all major US carriers** (AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon) as of February 1, 2025 — this is not optional guidance, it is carrier-level enforcement
- Any A2P (application-to-person) SMS sent from a business to customers/leads via a 10-digit long code must be registered through The Campaign Registry (TCR)
- Registration requires: legal business name, EIN exactly matching IRS records, brand registration + campaign registration
- Timeline: 1–3 weeks for approval
- Cost: nominal ($4–10/month depending on carrier fees)
- Fitness studios are eligible (not a restricted industry — restricted industries include cannabis, firearms, payday loans)

**For ARCH Training:** if they're sending automated SMS follow-ups (lead responses, appointment confirmations, re-engagement campaigns) through any platform (WellnessLiving, a CRM, etc.), 10DLC registration is a non-negotiable compliance requirement, not a nice-to-have.

## Claim 10: WellnessLiving / Mindbody Migration Data Hygiene

**Deck claim:** Common data problems when migrating from Mindbody to WellnessLiving.

### Verdict: CONFIRMED (from WellnessLiving's own documentation + community)

**Documented migration risks:**

WellnessLiving's official documentation and community forums confirm several recurring data issues:

1. **Duplicate client records** — WellnessLiving's own community forum thread "how to merge these duplicate client accounts" is a live thread from migrated users. They recommend merging duplicates in Mindbody _before_ migration, not after.

2. **Membership type mapping** — WellnessLiving documents that owned memberships, session passes, pricing, and date information can be migrated, but legacy membership structures in Mindbody often don't map 1:1 to WellnessLiving's schema. Custom/legacy types require manual review.

3. **Home location attribution** — WellnessLiving migrates "home location" as a data field. For single-location businesses this is simple; for any multi-location Mindbody account, location assignment for client records may need manual cleanup.

4. **Communication settings** — Opt-in/opt-out preferences for SMS and email marketing must be reviewed post-migration; Mindbody's consent model differs from WellnessLiving's.

5. **Migration freeze window** — WellnessLiving's rules state that certain items cannot be modified until the go-live date, adding risk if pre-migration data cleanup wasn't completed.

**WL-specific documentation is thinner than general CRM migration guidance** — the best source is WellnessLiving's own onboarding checklist and community forums, plus direct consultation with their onboarding specialist.

**Recommended deck language:** The deck should flag this as an implementation risk and recommend scheduling the WellnessLiving onboarding consultation early — specifically to audit membership type mapping and trigger a Mindbody duplicate-client merge before data export.

**Sources:**

- WellnessLiving migration docs: [wellnessliving.com/mindbody-migration](https://www.wellnessliving.com/knowledge-sharing/knowledge-base/mindbody-migration/)
- WL community duplicate accounts: [community.wellnessliving.com duplicate clients](https://community.wellnessliving.com/topic/how-to-merge-these-duplicate-client-accounts/)
- WL migration rules: [help.wellnessliving.com onboarding rules](https://help.wellnessliving.com/en/articles/9807200-rules-to-follow-when-onboarding-standard-data-migration)
